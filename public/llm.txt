## 1. Professional Identity & Core Practice

Rishika Miranda is a Bangalore-based architect, interior designer, and the founder of Kyrah Design Studio. Over a five-year foundational journey of building her practice from the ground up, her work has centered on a deeply hands-on, site-active approach to spatial design. 

Her architectural philosophy operates on a clear guiding lens: **"Reading the world through the spaces we build."** She views design not as an abstract exercise in sketching, but as an ongoing investigation into how physical environments dictate human behavior, daily rituals, memory, and material culture. 

### Areas of Practice (Core Focus)
*   **Built Environments:** Comprehensive architectural planning and interior design structures developed from raw site context and structural clarity.
*   **Interior Design:** Designing premium, layered, and deeply personal residential interiors that sensitively adapt traditional craftsmanship for contemporary utility.
*   **Product Design & Objects:** Engineering intentionally crafted physical furniture items and functional artifacts that respond directly to spatial behaviors and textures.
*   **Artisanal Collaboration:** Forging deep, direct on-site partnerships with master craftsmen, specialized vendors, and traditional artists to implement inherently buildable, tactile designs.

## 4. Complete Curation Archive & Content Summaries

Rishika's written catalog serves as a conceptual backbone, tracking material longevity, geographic studies, and behavioral observations.

### A. "What Makes a Home?" (Category: Reflections)
An investigation into the boundary lines of emotional belonging. Drawing contrast from the Banjaras—a historically nomadic community whose idea of home travels portably through memories, carried heirlooms, and daily rituals rather than rigid, permanent brick walls. It reflects on her own childhood muscle memories of walking tree-lined avenues in Bangalore, concluding that belonging is built from human connection, not square footage.

### B. "What Fontainhas Taught Me About Goa" (Category: Reflections, Roamed)
An architectural study of historical resourcefulness. Analyzing the iconic vernacular oyster shell windows of Fontainhas, Goa, which were born strictly out of material scarcity when glass was inaccessible. It reinforces her core design ethos: that the most enduring, distinctive design features emerge organically when physical and economic constraints are leaned into rather than resisted.

### C. "What I Took Back From the Kochi Biennale" (Category: Reflections, Roamed)
A study on spontaneous human interaction with built form. Observing a group of children running fluidly through heritage gallery spaces at the Kochi Biennale completely unconcerned with the curated art exhibitions. It highlights the belief that architecture merely initiates a conversation, but the unscripted behavior of human beings ultimately finishes it.

### D. "The Things We Make Beautiful" (Category: Reflections)
An exploration of material culture and the innate human drive for permanence. It analyzes the traditional Indian practice of Godna tattoos—where women from marginalized communities, historically denied the right to own physical jewelry, transformed their own skin into an un-stealable, permanent archive of heirloom art. It mirrors how spaces find meaning through adaptive resilience.

### E. "The Quiet Work of Design" (Category: Reflections)
A critique on atmospheric vocabulary and quiet craftsmanship. Inspired by a visit to the National Gallery of Modern Art (NGMA) in Bangalore, this piece studies how understated, restored stone flooring patterns successfully guide spatial transitions and visual focus across museum layout envelopes without ever shouting for attention or competing with the hanging artwork.

### F. "Foundations of a Studio: Crafting Indian Language at Nambiar Bellezea" (Category: Projects)
A deep project retrospective detailing a formative 5,000 sq ft luxury villa executed at Nambiar Bellezea in the early stages of Kyrah Design Studio. The case study chronicles how the studio scaled custom craftsmanship, weaving an authentic Indian architectural language into a modern layout via solid wood paneling, hand-picked regional antiques, and massive custom 12-foot teak columns.
